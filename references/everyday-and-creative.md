# Everyday questions, crafts, and creative projects

Treat 'how do I make a candle that looks like a bun?' as a complete and legitimate research task, just as an engineering question is. The user need not translate their goal into professional terminology. This example illustrates behavior; it does not request making or researching a candle every time the skill is used.

## Start from the intended result

Infer what the user wants to make, learn, choose, fix, or understand. Identify the few constraints that affect the approach: desired appearance, practical function, tools already owned, experience, budget, time, or available materials. Ask only consequential questions; where reasonable, state a beginner-friendly assumption and proceed. Do not impose professional standards or a commercialization plan on a personal experiment.

Convert informal descriptions into search vocabulary. For the candle example, potential discovery terms include 'bakery candle', 'bread-shaped candle', 'cinnamon roll candle', and native-language equivalents. Check whether results actually match the intended object rather than assuming every term is synonymous. Extend to languages associated with useful creators or techniques.

## Find visual references and complete procedures

Apply the social/media coverage rules, including Reddit and YouTube. For broad creative research, pursue TikTok, Instagram, Facebook, forums, image search, and relevant maker communities; add destinations such as Pinterest when they provide useful leads. Follow reposted images to the original tutorial or creator where possible.

Separate three kinds of material: inspiration showing a desired appearance, instruction showing how to obtain it, and specifications establishing how a particular material or tool should be used. A beautiful finished photograph is not proof that the manufacturing process is known or reproducible. A shop listing can identify a shape or material but is not automatically an independent tutorial.

Look for a procedure complete enough to reproduce: required materials, preparation, order of operations, important quantities or conditions, intermediate visual cues, finishing, and common failures. When a video skips a critical step, search for that step separately. Read maker discussions for problems and fixes, distinguishing first-hand trials from speculation.

## Break down the unfamiliar parts

Apply [method-breakdown.md](method-breakdown.md) to ordinary techniques as well as mathematical methods. Explain a material, tool, preparation, shaping process, or finishing technique when the user needs it to act. Use concrete language: what to get, what to do, why it matters, what it should look like, and what to try if it fails.

For the bun-shaped candle example, investigate rather than assume: possible shaping techniques; what creates the crust, spiral, or icing appearance; suitable materials and tools; and whether the intended object is decorative or will be burned. Compare realistic methods against beginner effort, appearance, equipment, and cost. Verify material compatibility and any use-specific precautions from suitable primary guidance. Do not invent temperatures, ratios, burn performance, or substitute compatibility from an attractive clip. Include only precautions relevant to carrying out the chosen procedure, at the step where they matter.

No need to derive chemistry or physics unless it explains an important choice or failure. Conversely, do not omit a crucial material property merely because the question sounds casual. Stop when the user can follow and check the process; do not equate a short question with a shallow answer.

## Deliver an approachable guide

Lead with the most suitable approach and why it fits the user's circumstances. Add a small set of visual references when useful and accessible, making clear what each demonstrates. Then give a materials/tool list with verified alternatives, ordered instructions, relevant approximate time/cost only when supported, and observable checkpoints. Explain unfamiliar terms in place.

Compare a few plausible alternatives when they change the result or effort. Separate aesthetic preference from factual claims about feasibility or performance. Clearly label any proposed combination of techniques as your adaptation rather than an already-tested recipe; suggest a small trial when that resolves meaningful uncertainty. Do not claim to have made or tested the object yourself.

Keep useful links near the steps and recommendations they support. A detailed version should add intermediate steps, visual cues, alternatives, and troubleshooting. Search logs and coverage belong after the practical answer, not in its way.
